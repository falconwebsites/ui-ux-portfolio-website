Making complex security workflows easier to manage.

A UX redesign for the Apache Ranger Policy Editor, focused on making policy creation easier to understand, configure and review without compromising the complexity enterprise users need.

Product Apache Ranger

01
About the product

Apache Ranger

Apache Ranger is an open-source security framework for managing and monitoring access to data across enterprise platforms.

It lets administrators define who can access which resources, what actions they can perform, and under what conditions through access policies.

02
The challenge

Making a powerful policy editor easier to use.

Ranger’s Policy Editor was powerful, but creating a policy was harder than it needed to be. Security administrators had to work through a dense form with multiple sections, fields, permissions, and configurations.

This made a task that administrators performed regularly feel slow, complex, and unnecessarily difficult to manage.

Design challenge

The challenge was to simplify the experience without taking away the flexibility and control enterprise users needed.

03
Understanding the problem

Before changing the interface, I needed to understand the task.

Policy management looks like a form on the surface, but underneath it is a decision-making workflow involving users, resources, permissions and conditions.

01

Who is struggling?

Administrators and technical users responsible for configuring and maintaining access policies.

02

What causes the friction?

A large number of configuration choices are presented together, making relationships between decisions harder to understand.

03

What is at stake?

Incorrect policies can affect who can access sensitive data and services.

04

What needs to change?

Reduce cognitive load while preserving the flexibility and control enterprise users require.

03
The approach

I treated the policy editor as a workflow problem, not a form-design problem.

Instead of starting with visual changes, I broke the experience into the decisions a user actually needs to make.

01

Understand

Understand the existing policy creation journey and terminology used by the product.

02

Structure

Break the task into logical groups rather than presenting everything as one large form.

03

Design

Create a clearer interaction model that makes each decision easier to understand.

04

Validate

Review the solution with stakeholders and iterate based on feedback and technical constraints.

04
Information architecture

The first decision was what belonged together.

The original experience presented all the fields together as one long form.

I reorganised them into logical groups based on how a policy is actually created — starting with service, security zone, and access type, followed by the resources that need to be secured and then the other policy settings.

This kept related decisions together and gave the configuration a more natural sequence.

05
Key design decision

Why a multi-step experience?

I first explored keeping the policy on a single page, reorganising the content into clearer groups. While this improved structure, users still had too much to process at once. A single long form gave users visibility into everything at once, but that visibility came at the cost of focus.

I then explored a step-based approach, where users could focus on one category of decision at a time while still understanding where they were in the overall policy creation journey.

Within these steps, I also looked at how much configuration needed to be visible at once. Where additional configuration was needed, sections such as Resources, Permissions, and Conditions offered an “Advanced settings” option. Users could expand these controls when needed, while the essential configuration remained visible by default.

This was less about making the interface feel simpler and more about making the user's mental workload manageable.

06
The solution

A policy creation experience built around decisions.

The final direction used a structured multi-step flow to separate the major decisions involved in creating a policy while maintaining a clear sense of progress.

Step 01

Define the Service

Start with the core policy information and establish the context before moving into access configuration.

Step 02

Define resources

Users can configure the relevant resources and access relationships without having to scan the entire policy at once.

Step 03

Configure permissions

Permissions and conditions are presented as a focused decision rather than another layer of information competing with the rest of the form.

Step 04

Review before saving

A final review stage gives users an opportunity to understand what they are about to create before committing the policy.

07
Collaboration

The design evolved through conversations, not in isolation.

Enterprise products are rarely designed by one person. Product requirements, technical constraints and stakeholder feedback all shaped the final direction.

Working with stakeholders

The important conversations happened around the decisions, not just the screens.

During reviews, I used the flows and prototypes to discuss whether the proposed structure matched how the product actually worked.

Feedback from the team helped surface product and implementation considerations that weren't obvious from the interface alone.

Those conversations helped me refine the interaction rather than treating the first design as the final answer.

09 / Outcome

A clearer way to work through a complex task.

The redesign shifted the experience from a dense configuration form toward a guided workflow, making the sequence of decisions easier to understand while preserving the depth required by enterprise users.

01 Reduced cognitive load
02 Clearer task progression
03 Better information hierarchy
04 More intentional interaction design
What I would measure

For a production rollout, I would evaluate task completion time, error rate, abandonment between steps, support questions and the confidence users have when reviewing policies before saving them.

10
What I learned

Good enterprise UX isn't about removing complexity.

Enterprise products often have genuine complexity. Trying to remove every field or decision can actually make the product less useful.

The more valuable UX problem is deciding how that complexity is introduced, grouped and communicated.

This project reinforced for me that strong UX is often about helping users build the right mental model of a system — before asking them to operate it.

01

Start with the user's decisions.

Forms are interfaces. Workflows are experiences. Understanding the latter creates better solutions.

02

Make complexity intentional.

Users can handle complexity when the interface gives them context and a clear sequence.

03

Design conversations matter.

Stakeholder and technical discussions are part of the design process, not something that happens after the design is finished.
