Futures & Options · Product Design

Making complex options data easier to understand.

An F&O trading platform designed to help traders make sense of Open Interest, OI changes, price movement and market strength without having to decode multiple screens of data.

Role UI/UX Designer
Focus Product Design · UX · UI
Domain Fintech · F&O
Platform Web Application
F&O DASHBOARD — HERO SCREENSHOT
01
The context

The data wasn't the problem. Understanding it was.

Futures & Options markets generate a huge amount of information. Open Interest, Change in OI, LTP, strike prices, Call and Put activity — all of it can be useful.

The challenge is that the information usually lives in separate tables, charts and indicators. A trader has to move between different pieces of data and mentally connect the dots.

I wanted to explore a simpler question:

The design question

How might we turn a large amount of options data into a clearer picture of what is happening in the market?

02
The challenge

Traders don't need more numbers. They need context.

The product had to expose the underlying data without making users feel like they were looking at a spreadsheet.

01

Too much information

OI, Change in OI, LTP and other values are useful individually, but become difficult to interpret when presented together without hierarchy.

02

Call and Put data need to be read together

Looking at only one side of the options chain doesn't provide the complete picture. The interface needed to make the relationship between CE and PE activity obvious.

03

Changes matter as much as the current value

A large Open Interest number tells one story. A large change in Open Interest can tell another. The UI needed to distinguish these signals clearly.

04

Users shouldn't have to decode the interface

The goal was not to hide the underlying market data. It was to organize it so that important relationships could be understood faster.

03
Understanding the data

Before designing the screens, I had to understand the signals.

The first part of the design work was understanding what each piece of data was actually telling the user.

Instead of treating every number equally, I grouped the information around the questions a trader would naturally ask:

OI

Where is the open interest?

Shows where positions are concentrated across different strike prices.

CHANGE IN OI

What is changing?

Highlights where new positions are being added or existing positions are being reduced.

PRICE

What is price doing?

Price movement adds another dimension to the interpretation of Open Interest activity.

CE / PE

Which side is more active?

Call and Put activity needs to be viewed together rather than as isolated numbers.

04
Information architecture

I structured the experience around decisions, not data.

Instead of starting with a giant options chain, I broke the experience into layers.

The most important information should be visible first. Detailed numbers could then support the user's understanding when they wanted to go deeper.

01

Market overview

Start with the broader market picture before diving into individual strikes.

02

Strike-level activity

Compare CE and PE activity around relevant strike prices.

03

Detailed data

Give users the underlying numbers when they need to verify or investigate a signal.

05
The options chain

One table. Multiple layers of information.

The options chain is where a large amount of information comes together, so hierarchy became especially important here.

I separated the Call and Put sides visually while keeping the Strike Price as the central reference point.

This lets the user scan horizontally to compare the two sides and vertically to understand activity across strikes.

06
Reducing the noise

Not every strike deserves equal attention.

One of the design challenges was helping users quickly identify the strikes where activity was concentrated.

Instead of forcing users to scan the entire chain, I introduced a focused view of the highest-interest strikes.

The Top 6 view surfaces the strongest current Open Interest concentrations while keeping the underlying options chain available for deeper analysis.

07
Strength summary

From raw activity to a readable signal.

One of the biggest opportunities was reducing the cognitive effort required to interpret multiple indicators together.

The Strength Summary brings the different pieces of activity together into a visual comparison between the Call and Put sides.

Instead of asking the user to compare several numbers manually, the interface provides a quick visual indication of where the combined activity is stronger.

STRENGTH SUMMARY SCREENSHOT

Design principle

Don't replace the data. Give the data hierarchy.

08
Visual language

The interface needed to feel analytical, not overwhelming.

Since the product deals with financial information, visual hierarchy had to communicate meaning without creating noise.

Clear hierarchy

Important numbers, strike prices and summary indicators receive stronger visual weight. Supporting information remains quieter.

CE / PE separation

Calls and Puts are visually separated so users can quickly understand which side they are looking at while still comparing both sides in the same context.

Progressive disclosure

The interface surfaces the most useful summary first and keeps deeper numerical information available underneath.

Minimal decoration

The visual design stays restrained because the data itself is already visually dense. Decoration should never compete with the information.

09
Design system

A system that could scale beyond one screen.

The product contains many data-heavy views, so consistency was critical.

I created reusable patterns for tables, cards, indicators, strike prices, summary states and data visualisation so that new screens could be added without reinventing the interface.

11
The outcome

A complex data product became easier to read.

The final design doesn't try to predict the market or hide the complexity of F&O data.

Instead, it gives that complexity structure.

See

Understand where Open Interest and activity are concentrated.

Compare

Read Call and Put activity in the same context.

Investigate

Move from a high-level summary into detailed strike-level data.

12
What I learned

Good fintech UX isn't about showing less data.

It is about helping people understand the data they are already looking at.

Working on the F&O product pushed me to think beyond visual design. I had to understand how different market indicators relate to each other and then translate those relationships into a visual hierarchy.

The biggest takeaway was simple:

When information is complex, the designer's job is not to simplify the information itself — but to simplify the way people navigate it.
